Open for business: Australia's 2.8 million businesses

Australia has more trading businesses than it has ever had, about one for every ten people. That total moves a lot from year to year. In 2025–26, about 460,000 businesses opened and 375,000 closed, and 14,153 companies failed owing debts they could not pay.

Part 1 · Who they are

Most Australian businesses are a single person

Say "business" and most people think of a shop with staff or an office. The typical Australian business is much smaller. It is a tradie with an ABN, a rideshare driver, a consultant working from home, or a family trust that owns a rental property. Nearly two in three businesses have no employees at all.

Small businesses rarely become big ones. The grid on the right follows every business that traded in both June 2025 and June 2026 and shows whether it hired, shed staff or stayed the same size. Read each row left to right: it starts at the business's size in 2025 and adds up to 100%.

What counts as a business?

The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) counts every business that has an Australian Business Number (ABN), is registered for GST, and is actively trading. A non-employing business is run by its owners only, with no paid staff. A business enters the count when it starts trading and exits when it stops, usually by cancelling its ABN.

Part 2 · Where they are

Seven in ten businesses are in a capital city

Seven in ten Australian businesses are registered in a capital city. Most people live in the capitals too, so a fairer comparison is the number of businesses per person. Measured that way, the inner-city business districts come out well ahead of everywhere else, and some farming regions rank higher than most suburbs.

Sydney's City and Inner South has 264 businesses for every 1,000 residents. Head offices and professional firms cluster there, which lifts the count well above what its resident population alone would produce. At the other end of the scale, the Northern Territory Outback has 38. Regions with many family farms, such as Darling Downs–Maranoa in Queensland (151 per 1,000), rank higher than most suburbs. Select Agriculture or Transport & delivery in the menu to see how differently those two industries are spread.

The fastest growth is on the edges of the big cities. Between June 2023 and June 2025, the regions that grew most were all outer suburbs, led by Blacktown (+13.9%), Perth South East (+13.7%), Adelaide North (+13.4%) and Melbourne West (+12.0%). In three of the top five, transport and delivery businesses, such as rideshare and parcel drivers, are now the largest industry.

Part 3 · Births and deaths

Hundreds of thousands of businesses open and close every year

Australia's business count has grown almost every quarter for a decade, from just under 2.0 million in 2013 to 2.7 million in 2025. The total gives no hint of the turnover behind it. Since 2023, around 125,000 businesses have started each quarter and around 110,000 have stopped.

Two periods look different from the rest. When COVID-19 lockdowns began in early 2020, closures fell, because government support such as JobKeeper kept many struggling businesses trading. A start-up boom followed in 2021. The September quarter of that year had 135,000 new businesses, the highest on record.

Part 4 · Industries

Carers and couriers are rising, farms are shrinking

The growth is uneven across industries. Since 2019, the number of health care businesses has risen by 61%. More carers, therapists and practitioners now work for themselves, a shift that has grown alongside the NDIS. Transport and delivery is up 37%. Agriculture is the only industry with fewer businesses than in 2019, as farms merge into larger holdings.

Transport and delivery has the highest turnover of any industry. In 2025–26, new businesses equal to 27% of the industry started and 22% closed. Only 37% of transport businesses that started in 2022–23 were still trading three years later, compared with 49% across all industries and 60% in agriculture. A driving business costs little to start, so people enter the industry easily and leave it just as easily.

The size of a typical business also varies by industry. In real estate and transport, more than 80% of businesses are sole operators, such as property investors holding a rental through a trust and drivers working for themselves. In hospitality, fewer than a third of cafés, restaurants and hotels run without staff.

Part 5 · When closing isn't a choice

Insolvencies have hit a record

Most closures are voluntary: the owner retires or decides to stop trading. A smaller number of businesses close because they cannot pay their debts, and that number is growing. When a company becomes insolvent, it enters external administration: an independent administrator or liquidator takes control. The corporate regulator, ASIC, counts every company this happens to.

During the pandemic, temporary debt relief and a pause in tax collection pushed insolvencies to their lowest level in more than two decades. That support then ended and the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) went back to chasing unpaid tax debts, and the companies that had been kept going began to fail. In 2024–25, 14,722 companies entered insolvency, the most ever recorded. The 2025–26 total of 14,153 was only slightly lower.

Putting the ABS and ASIC figures side by side shows which industries fail most often. Construction makes up 17% of all businesses but 25% of insolvencies. Hospitality is further out of proportion, with 4% of businesses and 15% of the companies that failed. Both industries run on thin margins, and builders are often locked into fixed-price contracts while their costs rise.

The states have moved apart as well. In 2018–19, insolvency rates were broadly similar across the eastern states. Since then, rates have risen sharply in New South Wales, Victoria, the Northern Territory and the ACT. Western Australia, helped by its resources economy, is the only state with a lower rate than before the pandemic.

Australia has never had more businesses, and in 2024–25 more companies failed than in any year on record.

The 2.8 million figure reflects more people working for themselves, particularly in care and delivery, and more businesses in the outer suburbs. Many of those businesses do not last. About half of new businesses close within three years, and insolvencies reached a record once pandemic support ended and costs rose. Anyone planning to start a business should expect that opening it will be easier than keeping it open.

How it was made

All charts and maps were built with Vega-Lite and embedded with Vega-Embed. Typefaces are Newsreader and Public Sans from Google Fonts. Maps use an Albers equal-area projection centred on Australia, so regions are shown at their true relative size. The city insets use a Mercator projection.

Use of AI

Generative AI (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help find and download the source datasets, write the data-processing script, draft the Vega-Lite specifications and page layout, and proof-read the text. All figures were checked against the original ABS and ASIC tables.
