A data story about the Australian economy

Open for business: Australia's 2.8 million businesses

Australia now has more trading businesses than ever, about one for every ten people. Behind that number is constant change. Every year, nearly half a million businesses open, more than a third of a million close, and a growing number fail with debts they cannot pay.

Part 1 · Who they are

Most Australian businesses are a single person

When we picture a business, we tend to imagine a shop with staff, an office, or a factory floor. The typical Australian business looks nothing like that. It is a tradie with an ABN, a rideshare driver, a consultant working from home, or a family trust that owns a rental property. Nearly two in three businesses have no employees at all.

What counts as a business?

The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) counts every business that has an Australian Business Number (ABN), is registered for GST, and is actively trading. A non-employing business is run by its owners only, with no paid staff. A business enters the count when it starts trading and exits when it stops, usually by cancelling its ABN.

Part 2 · Where they are

Business follows people, but not evenly

Seven in ten Australian businesses are registered in a capital city. That partly reflects where people live, so the fairer comparison is businesses per person. On that measure, the inner-city business districts stand far above everywhere else, and some farming regions rank surprisingly high.

Sydney's City and Inner South has 264 businesses for every 1,000 residents, many more than its residential population would suggest, because head offices and professional firms cluster there. At the other end, the Northern Territory Outback has 38. Regional areas with many family farms, such as Darling Downs–Maranoa in Queensland (151 per 1,000), rank higher than most suburbs. Try selecting Agriculture or Transport & delivery in the menu to see how differently industries are spread.

Growth is happening on the edges of the big cities. Between June 2023 and June 2025, the fastest-growing regions were all outer suburbs, led by Blacktown (+13.9%), Perth South East (+13.7%), Adelaide North (+13.4%) and Melbourne West (+12.0%). In three of the top five, transport and delivery businesses, such as rideshare and parcel drivers, are now the largest industry.

Part 3 · Births and deaths

A quiet, constant churn

Australia's business count has grown almost every quarter for a decade, from just under 2.0 million in 2013 to 2.7 million in 2025. The total hides how much movement sits underneath. Since 2023, around 125,000 businesses have started each quarter, and around 110,000 have stopped.

Two moments stand out. When COVID-19 lockdowns began in early 2020, closures fell rather than rose, because government support such as JobKeeper kept many struggling businesses alive. Then in 2021 came a record start-up boom: 135,000 new businesses in the September quarter of 2021 alone, the highest on record.

Part 4 · Winners and losers

Carers and couriers are rising, farms are shrinking

Not every industry shares in the growth. Since 2019, the number of health care businesses has risen by 61%, as more carers, therapists and practitioners work for themselves, a trend that has grown alongside the NDIS. Transport and delivery is up 37%. Agriculture is the only industry with fewer businesses today than in 2019, as farms consolidate into larger holdings.

Industries also differ in what their businesses look like. In real estate and transport, more than 80% of businesses are sole operators: property investors holding a rental through a trust, or drivers working for themselves. Hospitality is the opposite. Fewer than a third of cafés, restaurants and hotels run without staff.

Transport and delivery is Australia's most fluid industry. In 2025–26, new businesses equal to 27% of the industry started and 22% closed. Only 37% of transport businesses that started in 2022–23 were still trading three years later, compared with 49% across all industries and 60% in agriculture. Low start-up costs make it easy to start, and just as easy to stop.

Part 5 · When closing isn't a choice

Insolvencies have hit a record

Most closures are voluntary. An owner retires, moves on or simply stops trading. A small but growing number of businesses close because they can't pay their debts. When a company becomes insolvent, it enters external administration: an independent administrator or liquidator takes control. The corporate regulator, ASIC, counts every company this happens to.

During the pandemic, temporary debt relief and a pause in tax collection pushed insolvencies to their lowest level in more than two decades. When that support ended, and the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) resumed chasing unpaid tax debts, the backlog of struggling companies came through. In 2024–25, 14,722 companies entered insolvency, the most ever recorded. The pace eased only slightly in 2025–26.

Comparing the two data sources shows where financial distress is concentrated. Construction makes up 17% of all businesses but 25% of insolvencies. Hospitality is even more over-represented: 4% of businesses, but 15% of the companies that failed. Thin margins, rising costs and fixed-price contracts leave these industries little room when conditions turn.

The pattern across states has shifted too. In 2018–19, insolvency rates were broadly similar across the eastern states. Since then, rates have risen sharply in New South Wales, Victoria, the Northern Territory and the ACT, while Western Australia, helped by its resources economy, is now the only state lower than before the pandemic.

Australia has never had more businesses, and has rarely seen so many fail. Both are signs of an economy where starting out is easy and staying afloat is hard.

The 2.8 million figure is real progress: more people working for themselves, new industries forming around care and delivery, and outer suburbs filling with local enterprise. But half of new businesses close within three years, and record insolvencies show that rising costs and the end of pandemic support have caught up with many firms. For anyone thinking of starting a business, the data has a clear message: opening is the easy part.

How it was made

All charts and maps were built with Vega-Lite and embedded with Vega-Embed. Typefaces are Newsreader and Public Sans from Google Fonts. Maps use an Albers equal-area projection centred on Australia, so regions are shown at their true relative size. The city insets use a Mercator projection.

Generative AI (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help find and download the source datasets, write the data-processing script, draft the Vega-Lite specifications and page layout, and proof-read the text. All figures were checked against the original ABS and ASIC tables.
